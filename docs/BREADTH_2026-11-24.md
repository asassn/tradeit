# Breadth, measured — and the floor turns out to be thirteen arbitrary names

**Not a trial. Nothing registered, nothing graded, the ledger stays at 144.**
Reference numbers in the same category as §54 and `THE_FLOOR_2026-11-22.md`.
**No default changed and no published result moved**: every number is set per
arm on a local `StrategyConfig`, and the one arm that varies the ranker passes
it through `build_engine`'s existing optional `ranker=` argument, which exists
so the archive can be re-run under constraints without anything shifting.

Reproduce with `scripts/measure_breadth.py --sample 0..3`.

## Why this was run

`THE_FLOOR_2026-11-22.md` closes on a finding it records and does not act on:

> The structural finding — that the platform cannot hold a broad book, because
> twelve positions and no re-entry leave it in cash — is recorded here and not
> acted on.

Half of that is already answerable from the floor's own CSVs. `equal_weight`
re-offers the whole universe every 63 sessions, so re-entry *is* available to
it, and its exposure is 98.5%. **It is not sitting in cash.** What it is doing
is holding about a dozen names out of 500 offered.

So the question this run asks is the other half: *what happens when the book is
allowed to be wide?*

## Finding 1 — the floor is the twelve lowest instrument ids

This is the result that changes how every comparison on the scoreboard reads,
and it was not the result being looked for.

`EqualWeightBenchmark` offers every eligible name with `strength=1.0`, so every
candidate reaches the ranker with an **identical score**. `DiversityAwareRanker`
breaks ties on `(adjusted_score, -instrument_id)`, so the largest key wins and
the **smallest instrument id** is picked first. With a twelve-position cap, the
book is the twelve lowest-numbered instruments in the sample.

Measured on sample 0, the traded instrument ids are:

```
[1, 30, 904, 929, 966, 979, 994, 1027, 1031, 1037, 1039, 1047, 1052]
```

and the sample's twelve lowest ids are `[1, 30, 904, 929, 966, 979, 994, 1027,
1031, 1037, 1039, 1047]` — the first twelve, plus one replacement after a stop.
`securities_held` is 13 and the whole six-year book is those thirteen names.

**The confirmation is that the universe never mattered.** Re-run on the first
**40** securities of sample 0 instead of all 500, the arm returns 7.84% / 14.88%
max drawdown / Sharpe 0.448 on 35 trades and 13 names — **identical to the
published floor on the full 500**. A 500-name benchmark whose answer does not
change when 460 of the names are deleted was never measuring 500 names.

Instrument id is corpus load order. It carries no economic meaning, is not a
random draw, and is the same twelve names on every re-run.

So the published floor — **7.74%/yr at 16.66% maximum drawdown** — is the
six-year return of thirteen arbitrary securities. It is not a diversified
benchmark, and `THE_FLOOR`'s §2 attribution of delisting absorption to
*"diversification across the universe"* was comparing a thirteen-name book
against a thirteen-name screen: that comparison is about **selection**, not
breadth, and the breadth claim in it is not supported by the arms that produced
it.

**What this does not overturn.** The floor number is still the number the
platform produces when told to hold everything, which is what it was measured
for, and the scoreboard's rule — a candidate must beat the floor in its own
window — is unchanged. What changes is that nobody may describe the floor as a
diversified or broad-market benchmark, and the agreement with §37's RANDOM arm
(−0.03pp) is one coincidence between two draws, not evidence that the two
measure the same thing.

## Finding 2 — raising `max_positions` alone would have measured nothing

`max_positions` defaults to 12 and looks like the cap. It is not the only one.
`max_portfolio_heat_pct` is 0.06 and `risk_per_trade_pct` is 0.005, so the heat
rule binds at **exactly twelve positions too** — `StrategyConfig` computes that
quotient itself as `implied_max_positions`.

Raising one without the other leaves the other binding at twelve, and the run
would have reported *"breadth does not help"* having never widened the book.

Every arm here therefore holds the **risk budget constant and spends it across
more names**: heat stays 6%, `risk_per_trade_pct` becomes `0.06 / N`, and
`max_positions` becomes N. With a fixed 8% stop, notional per position is
`equity × (0.06/N) / 0.08`, which times N is **75% of equity at every N** — so
gross exposure is invariant and the only thing varying is how many names the
same risk is spread across. "More positions and more risk" would have
confounded breadth with leverage.

## Finding 3 — the book's ceiling is capital, not a risk rule

`SizingConfig.min_position_notional` is **$500** and refuses a position below it
rather than rounding up. Probed directly against the sizer at $100,000 of
equity:

| N | risk/trade | notional at $25 | at $50 | at $137 | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 0.00500 | $6,250 | $6,250 | $6,165 | ok |
| 25 | 0.00240 | $3,000 | $3,000 | $2,877 | ok |
| 50 | 0.00120 | $1,500 | $1,500 | $1,370 | ok |
| 100 | 0.00060 | $750 | $750 | $685 | ok |
| 150 | 0.00040 | $475 | $450 | $411 | **refused** |
| 200 | 0.00030 | $375 | $350 | $274 | **refused** |

So **the number of names this platform can hold is set by how much capital it
has**, not by any position-count or heat rule:

> max names ≈ capital × (heat ÷ stop_pct) ÷ min_position_notional

At $100,000 that is 150 nominally. **The demonstrated ceiling is 100**, and 150
is on the wrong side of the knife edge for a reason worth recording: `0.06/150`
is `0.00039999999999999996` in binary floating point, so the risk amount is
$39.999…, shares come to 19.9999… and floor to **19** rather than 20 — $475
instead of $500. A boundary that moves on the last bit of a float is not a
boundary to design against, which is why the general form above is stated as
approximate and the measured arms are reported as measured.

Holding 500 names would take roughly $333,000 of capital.

## The control reproduces the floor to the digit

Same four samples, same window, same costs, same delisting handling — and the

## Finding 4 — breadth buys nothing in return, and that is not a cost story

Four samples, recovery 1.0, means across samples:

| arm | names held | CAGR | range across samples | max drawdown | Sharpe | costs over 6y |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `breadth_012` | 11.9 | **7.74%** | 5.91% – 9.45% | 16.66% | 0.429 | $96 |
| `breadth_025` | 24.9 | 5.13% | 4.30% – 6.58% | 12.19% | 0.259 | $131 |
| `breadth_050` | 49.9 | 6.35% | 2.46% – 10.58% | 15.64% | 0.316 | $234 |
| `breadth_100` | 99.6 | **5.66%** | 3.32% – 8.80% | 13.02% | 0.299 | $452 |
| `breadth_150` | — | — | — | — | — | refused every position |
| `breadth_200` | — | — | — | — | — | refused every position |

**The narrow book's 2.08pp advantage does not survive its own dispersion.**
Paired on sample, twelve names minus one hundred:

| | mean | per sample | \|t\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| CAGR, recovery 1.0 | +2.08pp | +3.62, +3.13, +4.47, **−2.90** | **1.24** |
| CAGR, recovery 0.0 | +2.19pp | +3.43, +3.89, +5.35, **−3.91** | **1.05** |
| Sharpe, recovery 1.0 | +0.130 | +0.275, +0.094, +0.376, **−0.228** | 0.98 |

Sample 3 reverses the sign on every one of them. **Breadth has no measurable
effect on return at this capital, in either direction**, and the apparent
advantage of the narrow book is noise around an arbitrary twelve names.

**It is not costs.** Total execution cost over six years on $100,000 rises from
**$96** at twelve names to **$452** at a hundred — 0.016%/yr against
0.075%/yr, a difference of **0.06pp/yr** against a return gap of 2.08pp. Costs
account for about 3% of a gap that is itself indistinguishable from zero. The
`min_position_notional` floor and the $1 minimum commission are doing their job:
a hundred positions at $493 each is not a cost problem at this capital.

## Finding 5 — the one thing breadth demonstrably does buy

Under the **pessimistic delisting assumption** — every delisted holding written
to zero rather than to its last price — the narrow book's maximum drawdown is
**5.23pp deeper**, and unlike every other comparison here **all four samples
agree in sign**:

| | mean | per sample | \|t\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| max drawdown, recovery 0.0 | **+5.23pp** | +0.62, +7.00, +7.76, +5.54 | **3.26** |
| max drawdown, recovery 1.0 | +3.64pp | +0.34, +8.24, +9.10, **−3.12** | 1.22 |

So breadth does not improve returns, and it does do what diversification is
supposed to do: **it absorbs the write-off that a thirteen-name book cannot.**
That is the mechanism `THE_FLOOR` §2 described, now measured on breadth itself
rather than inferred from a comparison between two narrow books.

**This is not a pass and the \|t\| of 3.26 must not be read as one.** Nothing
here is registered, nothing is graded, the ledger stays at 144, and this is a
*drawdown* measurement rather than a hypothesis about returns. It is in sample,
on one window, four samples, in a strong equity market, and the recovery-1.0
column of the same comparison is flat. What it supports is a direction worth a
registration, not a result.

## Finding 6 — §54's diversification switch is a no-op on this path

§54 records:

> **Every portfolio-level result on this scoreboard was produced with
> diversification switched off**, by that line, uncommented […] turning it on
> changes what every published result means, which is a scoped proposition and
> not a default.

Read as a configuration decision, that invites turning the two weights up.
`diversify_012` does exactly that — `correlation_weight=0.5`,
`sector_penalty_per_holding=0.25`, deliberately large so a penalty that merely
rounded to nothing could not be mistaken for a penalty that cannot apply — and
the result is **identical to the control in all eight cells** (four samples ×
two delisting assumptions), to every digit of CAGR, drawdown, Sharpe, trade
count, cost and notional.

Two independent reasons, each a silent no-op rather than an error:

* **Correlation weighting cannot reach any backtest.**
  `DiversityAwareRanker.rank` takes a `correlation` matrix and `PortfolioCycle`
  passes one through, but `EventDrivenEngine` calls `cycle.plan` with **three
  arguments** (`backtesting/engine.py:397`). Every backtest ever run here ranked
  with `correlation=None`, so the weight multiplies nothing.
* **Sector penalties cannot reach these arms.** `_candidate` in
  `backtesting/baselines.py` builds every baseline candidate with
  `sector=None`, and `_penalty` charges nothing to a candidate with no sector.

**So "turn on diversification" is a change in two source files, not a
parameter**, and §54's sentence understates what acting on it would cost.

`tests/unit/test_diversification_reachability.py` pins both, so wiring either
path makes a test *fail* rather than quietly changing what every archived
number means. Four tests, four mutants, all caught — including one mutant that
survived the first version of the end-to-end test, recorded in that file because
the test passed against the exact change it existed to detect.

**Diversification's substance is not untested, and saying so would be wrong.**
§58 installed `SectorExposureRule` through `extra_rules` across 64 arms and
found a mean change **indistinguishable from zero** (+0.04%). That is the
risk-rule form, reachable and measured. This finding is about the ranker form
only.

## What this does and does not say

**Nothing here is a return result.** One window (2020-01-02 to 2025-12-31), four
samples, in sample, unregistered, on a corpus whose coverage is thinnest in its
oldest years. 2020–2025 was a strong equity market and every positive number
above inherits that.

**It is not a verdict on concentration.** The twelve-name arm is not a strategy;
it is whichever names the tie-break reached first. A finding that it beat a
broad book — which it did not, measurably — would have been a statement about
those twelve names, not about holding twelve of anything.

**The structural block `THE_FLOOR` recorded is real, and removing it as far as
this capital allows changed nothing measurable.** The book genuinely cannot
exceed about a hundred names at $100,000, and the cause is
`min_position_notional` rather than any position-count or heat rule. Widening it
from twelve to a hundred moved return by less than its own sampling error.

## What follows

**No change to any threshold, rule or default is proposed, and none was made.**

Three things are now decided rather than open:

1. **The floor may not be described as a diversified or broad-market
   benchmark.** It is thirteen names chosen by a tie-break. The scoreboard's
   rule — beat the floor in its own window — is unchanged, but the agreement
   between the floor and §37's RANDOM arm is a coincidence between two draws.
2. **Raising `max_positions` is not the lever**, and on its own it does nothing:
   heat binds at the same twelve. The lever is capital, and the relationship is
   `max names ≈ capital × (heat ÷ stop_pct) ÷ min_position_notional`.
3. **"Turn on diversification" is not a configuration change.** It is a change
   to `backtesting/engine.py` and `backtesting/baselines.py`, now pinned by
   tests, and it remains a scoped proposition.

The one open question this produced, and it is the owner's: **breadth reduced
the drawdown that delistings cause, consistently across four samples, while
costing nothing measurable in return.** Whether that is worth a registration —
trial 145, against a hurdle of |t| > 2.6563 — is a decision about how to spend a
trial, not an engineering one.
