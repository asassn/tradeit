# The floor, measured — and a premise of mine that was half wrong

**Not a trial. Nothing registered, nothing graded, ledger unchanged at 144.**
These are reference numbers in the same category as §54.

> **Addendum, 2026-11-24.** The numbers below reproduce exactly, and the
> structural finding at the end of this file was followed up. But **`equal_weight`
> is not "hold the universe"** — it holds thirteen names chosen by the ranker's
> tie-break on instrument id, and the identical result is obtained after deleting
> 460 of the 500 securities. See [`BREADTH_2026-11-24.md`](BREADTH_2026-11-24.md)
> before reading §2 or quoting the floor as a diversified benchmark.

## Why this was run

`BuyAndHold` and `EqualWeightBenchmark` have been in `backtesting/baselines.py`
since Phase 9 with twenty passing tests, and **no script had ever run them**.
`BuyAndHold`'s own docstring says what that costs:

> The floor every trading strategy has to clear. A rule that turns over
> constantly and cannot beat this has paid spread, commission and slippage for
> nothing — which is the comparison §44 through §53 never made, because they
> measured against a random security rather than against inaction.

I proposed this on the premise that every benchmark on the scoreboard is a
*random selection inside the platform*, so nobody had asked whether any of it
beats doing nothing.

## The premise was half wrong, and that is the first finding

**§37's RANDOM arm already was the floor, under a different name.**

| arm | s0 | s1 | s2 | s3 | mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `equal_weight` (hold the universe) | 7.84 | 9.45 | 7.79 | 5.91 | **7.74%** |
| §37 RANDOM (published) | 8.30 | 7.72 | 6.49 | 8.57 | **7.77%** |

A mean difference of **−0.03 pp**, each winning two samples of four. That is
not a coincidence and it should not have surprised me: a random 500 drawn from
2,806 is an unbiased estimate of the equal-weight universe, so §37's control
was already measuring inaction. The scoreboard's instruction — *"a signal worth
building must beat the better of the two in its own window"* — was therefore
always the right bar, and the bar was always the floor.

**What this run adds is that the number is now explicit, named, and reproducible
rather than implicit in a control.**

## What is genuinely new

### 1. The platform cannot express buy-and-hold

| arm | mean CAGR | mean exposure |
|---|---|---|
| `buy_and_hold` | **−0.22%** | **19.2%** |
| `buy_and_hold_timed` | −0.83% | 7.8% |
| `equal_weight` | 7.74% | 98.7% |

`MaxPositionsRule` caps the book at twelve. Told to buy 500 names, the platform
takes about fifteen, and because `BuyAndHold` never re-offers it **cannot
refill as positions stop out**. Average exposure across the four samples is
19.2%, ranging from 54.9% down to 3.8%. The arm spends most of the window in
cash and earns about nothing.

So "buy and hold" is not a thing this system can do, and the floor that *can*
be measured is **"hold the universe through the platform's risk machinery"**,
which is what `equal_weight` and §37's RANDOM both are. Stated because the
distinction decides what the number means.

### 2. The delisting assumption is where a screen gets punished

This is the result worth keeping.

| approach | recovery 1.0 | recovery 0.0 | cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| `equal_weight` (hold the universe) | 7.74% | 6.66% | **−1.09 pp** |
| §37 RANDOM (published) | 7.77% | 6.43% | −1.34 pp |
| **§37 CALM (low volatility)** | 3.31% | **−17.57%** | **−20.88 pp** |

Writing every delisted holding to zero instead of its last price costs a
diversified book about a point a year. It costs the low-volatility screen
**twenty-one points a year**, and takes its maximum drawdown to 70%.

**Diversification across the universe absorbs delisting risk; a screen
concentrates it.** §37 diagnosed the mechanism — the calm tail is partly
securities whose prices have stopped moving — and this quantifies what the
mechanism is worth against the alternative of not screening at all.

### 3. The one signal that predicts loses to the floor by 4.4 points a year

`realized_vol_60` passes a registered out-of-sample test as a *signal* (§35).
As a portfolio it earns **3.31%** against the floor's **7.74%**, at recovery
1.0, on the same samples and window. At recovery 0.0 the gap is 24 points.

### 4. The time stop costs return and buys drawdown, again

`equal_weight` 7.74% / 16.66% max drawdown; `equal_weight_timed` 6.96% /
15.53%. About 0.8 pp of return for about 1.1 pp of drawdown — the same trade
§38 found for the stop ladder, in the same direction and smaller.

## What this does not say

**Nothing here is a return result.** One window (2020-01-02 to 2025-12-31),
four samples, in sample, unregistered, on a corpus whose coverage is thinnest
in its oldest years. 2020–2025 was a strong equity market and every positive
number above inherits that.

**The arms are not identical to §37's.** Its `FactorTilt` applied a
per-rebalance eligibility filter — `min_price` $5, `min_dollar_volume` $1M,
`max_atr_percent` 1.0, `min_history` 100. `BuyAndHold` and
`EqualWeightBenchmark` carry only a stop; `_StopFromPercent` has no liquidity
parameters at all, so they offer every name in the universe every time. The
population was filtered for liquidity across 2019, so what is missing is the
*ongoing* filter — and the direction matters: **these floor arms hold names
§37's arms would have dropped**, so the floor is less protected than what it
is read against, not more.

Sizing matches: a fixed 8% stop makes the entry-to-stop distance proportional
to price, so risk-based sizing produces a constant notional — the equal-dollar
sizing §37 used, reached the same way. The sub-penny catastrophe §38 found
cannot recur, because `RiskBasedSizer` now refuses any candidate whose round
trip costs more than 1% of its notional.

## What follows

**No change to any threshold, rule or default is proposed.**

The practical consequence is for how a future result is read, and it is already
the scoreboard's own rule: a candidate must beat the floor in its own window,
and the floor on 2020–2025 is **7.74%/yr at a 16.7% maximum drawdown**, falling
to 6.66% if delisted holdings are worth nothing.

The structural finding — that the platform cannot hold a broad book, because
twelve positions and no re-entry leave it in cash — is recorded here and not
acted on. Raising `max_positions` or re-offering held names changes what every
backtest measures, which is a scoped proposition and the owner's to make.

Reproduce with `scripts/measure_the_floor.py --sample 0..3`.

## Addendum, 2026-11-24 — what the follow-up found, and what it corrects

Measured in [`BREADTH_2026-11-24.md`](BREADTH_2026-11-24.md). Every number in
this file reproduces to the digit — 7.74% / 16.66% at recovery 1.0 and 6.66% at
recovery 0.0 — so nothing here is withdrawn. Three things are qualified.

**1. `equal_weight` holds thirteen names, not the universe.** Every candidate it
offers scores `strength=1.0`, so they all reach the ranker tied, and
`DiversityAwareRanker` breaks ties on `-instrument_id`. The book is therefore the
**twelve lowest instrument ids** in each sample — on sample 0, exactly
`[1, 30, 904, 929, 966, 979, 994, 1027, 1031, 1037, 1039, 1047]` plus one
replacement. Instrument id is corpus load order and carries no economic meaning.
The confirmation: re-run on the **first 40** securities instead of all 500 and the
arm returns 7.84% / 14.88% / Sharpe 0.448 on 35 trades and 13 names — identical.
**A benchmark whose answer does not change when 460 of its names are deleted was
never measuring 500 names.**

So the phrase *"hold the universe"* used throughout this file is literally about
intent — the arm offers the universe — and not about what was held. The floor
remains the number the platform produces when told to hold everything, which is
what it was measured for, and the scoreboard's rule is unchanged. It may not be
described as diversified or broad-market.

**2. §2's breadth attribution is not supported by the arms that produced it.**
*"Diversification across the universe absorbs delisting risk; a screen
concentrates it"* compared a thirteen-name `equal_weight` book against a
thirteen-name low-volatility screen. Both are narrow, so that comparison is about
**selection**, not breadth. Measured on breadth directly, the mechanism **does**
hold: a hundred-name book's maximum drawdown is **5.23pp shallower** than a
twelve-name book's under recovery 0.0, with all four samples agreeing in sign.
The conclusion survives; the evidence for it in this file did not establish it.

**3. The structural finding was half wrong, and the half that was right has a
different cause.** *"Twelve positions and no re-entry leave it in cash"* is
accurate for `buy_and_hold` and not for `equal_weight`, which re-offers every 63
sessions and runs at 98.5% exposure. And raising `max_positions` would have
changed nothing: heat 6% ÷ risk 0.5% binds at twelve by a second route. The real
ceiling is `min_position_notional` at $500 — about **a hundred names at
$100,000** — so breadth is limited by capital, not by a risk rule. Widening the
book to a hundred moved return by less than its own sampling error (|t| 1.24).
